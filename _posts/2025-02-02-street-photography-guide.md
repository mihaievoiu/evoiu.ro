## Getting Started

The hardest part of street photography isn't technical — it's psychological. Most beginners are afraid of pointing a camera at strangers, worried about reactions, conflict, or simply feeling intrusive.

Here's what I wish someone had told me at the beginning: most people don't notice you. And those who do are usually curious, not hostile.

## The Right Gear

You don't need much. I've shot some of my favourite images with a small compact and a 35mm lens. The best street camera is one that's unobtrusive — small, quiet, and without a loud shutter.

What matters more than gear is how you carry yourself. Confidence reads as belonging. If you look like you know what you're doing, most people assume you have a reason to be there.

## Getting Close

Henri Cartier-Bresson had a rule: if your photos aren't good enough, you're not close enough. It's still true.

Wide lenses — 28mm, 35mm — force you to get physically close to your subjects. That proximity creates intimacy and connection. It's uncomfortable at first. It gets easier.

## Developing an Eye

Walk the same streets repeatedly. You begin to understand how light falls at different times of day, where interesting people gather, what's likely to happen where. Street photography rewards attention.

Look for contrast: old and new, light and shadow, stillness and motion. Look for moments about to happen — the decisive moment Cartier-Bresson described, where all the elements align for a fraction of a second.

> Then press the shutter.

Hesitation costs you the frame. Trust your instincts, review later.
